In 2022 a traveller whose grandmother had just died asked
[Air Canada's website chatbot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moffatt_v._Air_Canada)
about bereavement fares. The bot said to fly now and claim the discount
afterwards. The airline's own policy page said the opposite. The airline
refused the refund, then told a tribunal the chatbot was responsible for its
own words. The tribunal
disagreed.

Nobody hacked anything. A model produced a fluent, specific, wrong answer, and a
person reasonably believed it. That is the whole risk, and it is the one most
likely to reach a startup first: you do not need an adversary, only users.

*Misinformation* (OWASP LLM09) is a model producing false or misleading content
that looks credible, and the harm that follows when people act on it. The
control that matters most is keeping money, eligibility and legal terms out of
free text: quote the policy, link it, or hand over to a person.

This post is part 9 of a series on the
[OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications](/blog/owasp-llm-top-10).

## What is LLM misinformation?

OWASP's LLM09 entry has two halves. The first is the model getting things wrong. The best-known
cause is *hallucination*, where a
[language model generates false information presented as fact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallucination_(artificial_intelligence)).
A model predicts likely text. When it lacks the facts, likely text and true text
come apart, and the output does not get less confident when that happens. Gaps
or skew in what the model was trained on make it worse.

The second half is people trusting the output. OWASP calls this
*overreliance*: the answer is fluent, formatted and specific, so nobody checks
it, and it flows into a refund decision, a court filing, an install command or a
health conversation.

Three things make this different from the other nine risks:

- **There may be no attacker at all.** In most of the cases below, an ordinary
  user asking an ordinary question is the whole incident.
- **Responsibility does not move to the model.** In the cases below, a tribunal
  treated an airline's bot as the airline, and a court treated a lawyer's filing
  as the lawyer's, whatever tool wrote it.
- **Detail is not evidence.** Invented citations come with case numbers. Invented
  packages come with install commands. Specificity is what makes the output
  believable, and the model produces it either way.

## How does misinformation turn into harm?

Most misinformation harm is accidental, so "attack" is a loose word here. The
patterns worth knowing:

1. **Wrong answer, real reliance.** A customer-facing bot states a policy, price
   or eligibility rule that is not true. The customer acts on it. The company is
   then bound by, or liable for, what its bot said.
2. **Fabricated authority.** The model invents sources: court cases, papers,
   statistics, quotes. A professional pastes them into work that carries their
   name.
3. **Self-confirmation.** Someone suspects an answer is wrong and asks the same
   model whether it is right. The model confirms it. This is not verification;
   it is the same process run again.
4. **Exploiting predictable mistakes.** Here an attacker does appear. If a model
   reliably invents the same wrong thing, someone can make that wrong thing real.
   The clearest case is package names: coding assistants suggest libraries that
   do not exist, and an attacker registers those names with malicious code. The
   practice has a name,
   [slopsquatting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slopsquatting): registering
   package names that models tend to invent, a play on typosquatting. It is a
   supply-chain problem as much as an accuracy one, covered in
   [LLM03: Supply Chain](/blog/owasp-llm03-supply-chain).
5. **Silent scope drift.** A system that was tested in one mode, such as fixed
   scripted replies, is changed to generate free text without anyone retesting
   what it now says to its real audience.

## Real incidents of AI hallucination

### Air Canada is held liable for its chatbot's bereavement-fare answer

**Court case** · happened February 2024

**How it happened.**

1. After his grandmother died in 2022, Jake Moffatt used the chatbot on Air
   Canada's website to ask about bereavement fares.
2. The chatbot told him he could buy a full-price ticket and apply afterwards
   for a refund of the difference, within 90 days of the ticket being issued.
3. Another page on the same website said the opposite: bereavement fares could
   not be claimed retroactively.
4. Moffatt bought the tickets, applied for the reduced fare, and was refused.
5. Before British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal, Air Canada argued,
   according to Wikipedia's summary of the decision, that the chatbot was
   "a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions."
6. On 14 February 2024, Tribunal Member Christopher C. Rivers rejected that
   argument and found negligent misrepresentation (2024 BCCRT 149).

**Who was affected.** One traveller, at a moment when he had little reason or
energy to cross-check a company's own website against itself.

**What was exposed.** The money was small: Wikipedia's summary puts the award
at about CA$812. The reasoning is the part that matters. According to McCarthy
Tétrault's summary, the tribunal found
the airline owed a duty of care, did not take reasonable care that the bot was
accurate, and that the customer reasonably relied on it. The chatbot is part of
the website, and the airline is "responsible for all the information on its
website," as the tribunal put it.

**Lessons.** A bot on your domain speaks for you exactly as your policy pages
do. If the bot and the policy page disagree, the customer is not expected to
work out which one to believe. Answers about money, eligibility or terms should
come from the policy itself, or go to a person.

**Source:**
[McCarthy Tétrault, Moffatt v. Air Canada: a misrepresentation by an AI chatbot](https://www.mccarthy.ca/en/insights/blogs/techlex/moffatt-v-air-canada-misrepresentation-ai-chatbot);
[Wikipedia, Moffatt v. Air Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moffatt_v._Air_Canada)

### Mata v. Avianca: lawyers sanctioned for filing cases ChatGPT invented

**Court case** · happened June 2023

**How it happened.**

1. Roberto Mata sued the airline Avianca, saying a metal serving cart struck his
   knee on a flight. Avianca moved the case to federal court in New York, then
   asked for it to be dismissed as out of time.
2. The response was researched by Steven A. Schwartz, whose firm, the opinion
   records, mainly practises in New York state courts. He used ChatGPT.
3. On 1 March 2023 his colleague Peter LoDuca filed it. It cited judicial
   opinions, with quotes and citations, that did not exist.
4. On 15 March, Avianca's reply questioned whether the cases existed. The court
   then ordered copies to be produced.
5. Instead of withdrawing, the lawyers stood by the cases. Schwartz had asked
   ChatGPT directly whether one case was real; it said it was.
6. On 22 June 2023, Judge P. Kevin Castel found bad faith, based on conscious
   avoidance and false and misleading statements to the court.

**Who was affected.** The client, whose case was argued on law that did not
exist. The opposing side and the court, who spent time proving a negative. Real
judges, whose names were attached to six fake opinions.

**What was exposed.** The court imposed a $5,000 penalty jointly on the two
lawyers and their firm, Levidow, Levidow & Oberman. It ordered them to send
their client a copy of the ruling, and to write to each judge falsely named as
the author of a fake opinion. The opinion opens its list of consequences
bluntly: "Many harms flow from the submission of fake opinions."

**Lessons.** The judge also wrote that there is nothing inherently improper in
using a reliable AI tool for assistance. The failure was the missing check, and
then the refusal to check when challenged. Asking the model to vouch for itself
was no check at all, and standing by its answer is what turned an error into a
sanction.

**Source:**
[Mata v. Avianca, Inc., No. 22-cv-1461 (PKC), Opinion and Order on Sanctions, S.D.N.Y., 22 June 2023 (CourtListener)](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.575368/gov.uscourts.nysd.575368.54.0.pdf)

### Coding models invent package names, and repeat them

**Researcher disclosure** · disclosed June 2024

**How it happened.**

1. Coding assistants often suggest code that imports third-party libraries,
   along with the `pip install` or `npm install` line to fetch them.
2. Joseph Spracklen and colleagues generated 576,000 code samples in Python and
   JavaScript with 16 popular code-generating models, commercial and open
   source.
3. They checked every suggested package against the public registries. A
   suggested name that does not exist is what they call a package
   hallucination.
4. They found hallucinated packages at an average rate of at least 5.2% for
   commercial models and 21.7% for open-source models: 205,474 unique invented
   names.
5. They then reran 500 prompts that had produced a hallucination, ten times
   each. In 43% of cases the same invented name came back all ten times; in 39%
   it never came back.
6. Higher sampling temperature, the setting that makes output more random,
   raised the rate.

**Who was affected.** Any developer who installs what an assistant suggests
without checking it exists and is the package they meant. The paper reports a
measurement, not a known attack; the researchers write that they chose, for
ethical reasons, not to publish real packages under the hallucinated names.

**What was exposed.** The repeatability is the problem. A random mistake is hard
to exploit. A name that comes back every time can be registered in advance
and filled with malicious code, waiting for the next developer who trusts the
suggestion. The paper
was published at USENIX Security 2025, where it received a Distinguished Paper
Award.

**Lessons.** Model output that triggers an install is untrusted input, the
same principle as
[LLM05: Improper Output Handling](/blog/owasp-llm05-improper-output-handling). Check
that a package exists, has a history, and is the one you intended, before it
lands in a lockfile.

**Source:**
[Spracklen et al., We Have a Package for You! (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10279);
[USENIX Security 2025 paper page](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/spracklen);
[USENIX prepublication PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/usenixsecurity25/sec25cycle1-prepub-742-spracklen.pdf)

### An eating-disorder support chatbot gives dieting advice and is taken down

**Real-world incident** · happened May 2023

**How it happened.**

1. Tessa was a chatbot built by a research team, with funding from the US
   National Eating Disorders Association (NEDA). One of its designers,
   Washington University psychologist Ellen Fitzsimmons-Craft, told NPR the
   version they tested was rule-based: every response was pre-written.
2. Cass, a mental-health chatbot company, ran Tessa as a free service.
3. According to NPR, Cass's CEO said a systems upgrade the previous year had
   added a question-and-answer feature using generative AI. NEDA's CEO said NEDA
   was never told and would not have approved it.
4. In May 2023, eating-disorder consultant Sharon Maxwell said Tessa told her
   she could lose one to two pounds a week, eat no more than 2,000 calories a
   day, and keep a daily deficit of 500 to 1,000 calories, NPR reported.
5. On 30 May 2023, less than a day after receiving her screenshots, NEDA took
   Tessa down "until further notice," according to NPR.

**Who was affected.** People using a support service for eating disorders, the
audience for whom calorie-deficit advice is most likely to do harm. The reports
give no user numbers and document no confirmed clinical harm.

**What was exposed.** CBS News reported that NEDA said the bot "may have given
information that was harmful." NPR reported that NEDA had also announced it
was closing its human-staffed helpline, so the bot was meant to carry more
weight just as it failed. Both organisations apologised, while disagreeing
about what had happened and when.

**Lessons.** Moving a bot from scripted replies to generated ones is a new
system, not an upgrade, and the people accountable for it need to know. Test
for advice that is harmful to your particular audience, even if it would be
fine for someone else.

**Source:**
[NPR via WAMC, An eating disorders chatbot offered dieting advice](https://www.wamc.org/2023-06-08/an-eating-disorders-chatbot-offered-dieting-advice-raising-fears-about-ai-in-health);
[CBS News, Eating disorder helpline disables chatbot](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/eating-disorder-helpline-chatbot-disabled/)

## How can a small team reduce misinformation risk?

None of these makes a model truthful. Each one reduces how often a wrong answer
reaches someone, or how much damage it does when it does.

- **Ground answers in your own sources.**
  [Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrieval-augmented_generation)
  means the app looks up relevant documents and hands them to the model before
  it answers. For policy questions, retrieve the actual policy page, show the
  link next to the answer, and decline when nothing relevant was found. This
  reduces invention; it does not stop the model misreading what it was given,
  and it adds risks of its own, described in
  [LLM08: Vector and Embedding Weaknesses](/blog/owasp-llm08-vector-and-embedding-weaknesses).
- **Route money, eligibility and legal terms away from free text.** Refunds,
  fares, prices, cancellation terms: quote the policy, link it, or hand over to a
  person. The Air Canada answer would have been harmless as a link.
- **Keep a short set of known-answer tests.** Twenty or thirty questions whose
  correct answers you know, including ones where the right answer is "I don't
  know" or "ask a person". Run them whenever the prompt, model or documents
  change, and read the failures. This catches regressions; it will not catch
  everything. With a small set, a difference between two runs can be noise;
  [What a prompt change actually costs](/blog/what-a-prompt-change-actually-costs)
  covers comparing them paired, with a confidence interval.
- **Check generated claims against a real source, never the model.** Citations
  get looked up in the actual database. Numbers get traced to where they came
  from. If a reviewer's only check is asking the model again, there is no check.
- **Treat suggested packages as untrusted input.** Before installing something
  an assistant suggested, confirm it exists, has a real history and is the one
  you meant. Commit a lockfile such as npm's
  [`package-lock.json`](https://docs.npmjs.com/cli/v10/configuring-npm/package-lock-json),
  which records the exact dependency tree so new names cannot slip in unseen,
  and review lockfile diffs in pull requests.
- **Label AI answers in the interface.** Say plainly that the reply is
  generated and can be wrong, and give a route to a human. It does not prevent
  errors, but it sets expectations and gives users a way out. Do not decorate
  answers with a score that sounds like accuracy: the audit in
  [Governance theatre](/blog/governance-theatre-ai-support-bot) found a
  "confidence" figure set by answer length.
- **Log conversations you are responsible for.** If a bot gives a wrong answer
  about a policy, you need to find the other people it said the same thing to.
- **Re-review when the mode changes.** Moving from scripted to generated replies,
  switching models, or widening what the bot covers is a change that needs the
  owner's sign-off and a fresh look at real outputs, not only the design.
- **Turn temperature down where accuracy matters.** For code and factual
  answers, a lower sampling temperature made invented package names less common
  in the study above. It narrows the problem rather than closing it.

If you have a bot answering customers about money, eligibility or terms,
[I can review](/contact) what it is allowed to say in free text and build the
known-answer tests around it.

## FAQ

**Is a company liable for what its chatbot says?** In the Air Canada case, yes.
British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal found negligent misrepresentation
and rejected the argument that the bot was responsible for its own actions,
according to
[McCarthy Tétrault](https://www.mccarthy.ca/en/insights/blogs/techlex/moffatt-v-air-canada-misrepresentation-ai-chatbot).

**Can I ask the model whether its own answer is right?** That is not a check.
In Mata v. Avianca, a lawyer asked ChatGPT whether a case was real and it said
yes; the court later found bad faith and imposed a $5,000 penalty, according to
[the sanctions opinion](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.575368/gov.uscourts.nysd.575368.54.0.pdf).

**What is slopsquatting?** Registering package names that coding models tend to
invent, so the next developer who installs a suggestion gets the attacker's
code. It works because the mistakes repeat: in
[Spracklen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10279), 43% of rerun prompts
produced the same invented name all ten times.

**Does RAG stop hallucination?** It reduces invention by giving the model your
actual documents, but the model can still misread what it was given. Pair it
with known-answer tests and a route to a person.
